description: Simulate Kafka consumer group rebalances on a schedule to test how your consumers behave during eager rebalancing, with Khaos.

A consumer group rebalance is disruptive by nature: when membership changes, every consumer in
the group (not just the one that joined or left) pauses while partitions get reassigned. Whether
your consumers handle that cleanly, with no double-processing, no dropped in-flight work, and a fast
recovery, is hard to know until a rebalance actually happens. Khaos triggers real rebalances on a
schedule so you can find out deliberately.

<Terminal
  lines={[{ type: "command", text: "khaos run chaos/rebalance-storm" }]}
/>

## Triggering a real rebalance

The `rebalance_consumer` incident closes one matched consumer entirely (a genuine departure from
the group, not a pause) and then registers a fresh replacement. That departure and rejoin is what
actually causes the group coordinator to rebalance:

```yaml
incidents:
  - type: rebalance_consumer
    every_seconds: 20
    initial_delay_seconds: 15
```

`target:` (with `topic`, `group`, and `percentage` or `count`) narrows which consumer gets picked,
if you have more than one group and want to isolate the effect. See
[Scenario File Reference](/docs/reference/scenario-file#incidents) for the full incident schema.

## Why it's disruptive by design

Khaos's consumer groups use eager `Range`/`RoundRobin` assignment rather than cooperative-sticky,
a deliberate choice, since it's what makes rebalance testing here meaningful: every consumer in the
group stops processing while assignment is recomputed, not just the consumer that changed. If your
production consumers use cooperative-sticky assignment instead, a Khaos-triggered rebalance will be
more disruptive than what you'd see in production, useful as a worst-case test, but worth knowing
going in.

## A gap worth knowing about

The replacement consumer that Khaos creates after a `rebalance_consumer` incident reverts to
default `failure_rate`, `commit_failure_rate`, `on_failure`, and `max_retries`. It does not inherit
whatever failure-simulation config the original consumer had. If you're specifically testing the
interaction between failure simulation and rebalancing, account for that rather than assuming
config carries over.

## What to test

- **In-flight work during a rebalance.** Does a consumer that was partway through processing a
  batch handle losing its partition assignment cleanly, or does it double-process after
  reassignment?
- **Recovery time.** How long after a rebalance completes before consumption resumes at normal
  throughput, and does lag from that gap get worked off, or does it linger?
- **Offset commit timing relative to rebalance.** A consumer that commits infrequently has more to
  reprocess (or lose, depending on commit strategy) when a rebalance interrupts it mid-batch.
